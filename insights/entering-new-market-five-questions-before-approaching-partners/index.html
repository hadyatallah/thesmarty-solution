Entering a new market: five questions to answer before approaching partners

Before approaching potential partners, clarify your target customer, test local demand, choose a workable sales route and check whether your team can deliver. These five questions provide a practical starting point.

By The Smarty Solution

1. Whose problem are we solving?

Choose a specific customer group and describe the problem you expect to solve. “Businesses in Cyprus” is too broad to guide a useful market test. Narrow the segment by sector, size, location or buying situation.

Then examine how those customers manage the problem today. What would make them consider changing? Who uses the solution, who approves the purchase and who controls the budget? A clear answer helps you identify which capabilities a prospective partner should bring.

2. What evidence supports local demand?

Treat success elsewhere as a starting assumption to test locally. Review competing offers, available market information and the alternatives customers already use. Where appropriate, speak directly with potential buyers about their priorities, purchasing process and current frustrations.

Look for evidence beyond positive reactions: a defined requirement, a realistic budget or willingness to explore a limited trial. Record what remains uncertain. Set a threshold for continuing, such as enough qualified demand to justify the next stage of research.

3. What offer and sales route are we testing?

Decide what you intend to sell first, to whom and through which route. Direct sales, distribution and referral arrangements place different demands on your pricing and delivery model.

Work through the economics before seeking a partner. Allow for delivery, support, sales costs and any proposed partner margin. Consider whether packaging, language, training or service levels need adjustment. Give each possible route a clear purpose, then choose a manageable starting point that your team can evaluate.

4. Can we support the first customers properly?

Map the work from an initial enquiry through delivery and after-sales support. Identify who will prepare quotations, handle technical questions, manage onboarding and resolve problems. Check whether your team has the time, systems and budget to fulfil those responsibilities.

Build a modest test around your actual capacity. Define its duration, spending limit and measures of progress. Decide what would justify expanding, changing the offer or stopping. This keeps early commitments aligned with what the business can reliably deliver.

5. What needs specialist review before we commit?

List the assumptions that could affect whether your proposed model is workable. Depending on the product, service and market, these may involve licensing, product requirements, contracts, employment, tax or data handling.

Ask appropriately qualified local professionals to assess the relevant questions before making commitments that depend on the answers. Keep the commercial plan and specialist findings connected: if advice changes the viable route or expected costs, update your test accordingly.

Bring the answers together in a short market-entry plan, with evidence, open questions and clear decision points. You can then assess prospective partners against the practical job that needs doing.
