From enquiry to next step: a simple follow-up process for small businesses

Give every business enquiry a clear owner, a useful next step and an accurate status. This practical process helps small teams follow through on promises and decide which conversations should progress, pause or close.

By The Smarty Solution

A potential customer asks a question, a colleague promises to send details, and the conversation moves between email, calls and messages. Keeping track becomes difficult when the next action lives only in someone’s memory. A simple follow-up process gives a small team a shared view of what needs attention and why.

1. Capture the essentials

Create one record for each enquiry in a shared tracker or the customer-management tool you already use. Record the contact’s name, company, enquiry date, source, relevant contact details and a short description of what they need.

Include any preferred communication channel and timing they have mentioned. Keep notes focused on the business conversation and avoid collecting unrelated personal information. Link to the original exchange so colleagues can check the context without reconstructing it.

2. Understand the need before proposing a solution

Clarify the outcome the person wants, the problem they are trying to solve and any practical constraints. Ask about timing, scope and budget where those details help establish whether your business can assist.

Separate what they have confirmed from what your team still assumes. A request for prices may need one clear answer; a broader project may need a short discovery conversation. Match the next step to the actual enquiry.

3. Give one person responsibility

Assign an owner who can see the conversation through. Other colleagues can contribute, but the owner remains responsible for checking that promised work happens and the record stays current.

If responsibility changes, make the handover explicit. Record who is taking over, what has been discussed and what is still outstanding. Check that the new owner has accepted the handover before treating it as complete.

4. Agree a concrete next step

Replace vague notes such as “follow up” with a specific action: send the requested specification, answer an open question or arrange a discussion about scope. Record who will act and the timing actually agreed.

If timing has not been discussed, mark it as unconfirmed and clarify it when appropriate. An internal reminder to review the record should remain distinct from a commitment made to the customer. Do not invent a deadline on their behalf.

5. Keep the status meaningful

Use a small set of statuses your team understands, such as new, assessing, awaiting our action, awaiting customer, paused and closed. Add a brief reason when pausing or closing an enquiry.

Respect a decline or request to stop contact. Where someone asks to revisit the conversation later, record the timing and any conditions they specify. Repeated messages without a useful purpose can add work for both sides.

6. Review what is outstanding

Choose a regular review rhythm that suits the enquiry volume. Check for unanswered questions, missed promises, unclear ownership and records whose status no longer reflects the conversation.

Start by improving those basics. Once the process is clear, you can assess whether your existing tools support it adequately and where a specific improvement would help.
